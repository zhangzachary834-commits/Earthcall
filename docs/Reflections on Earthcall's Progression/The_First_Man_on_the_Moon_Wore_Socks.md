# The First Man on the Moon Wore Socks

*A correction to the parish almanac, with specimens*

The first intelligible message our observatory received from the Moon was **WHERE DOES IT HURT**. This considerably narrowed the field of possible civilizations.

Eleven-year-old June Mercado had made the Moon in Earthcall for the Christmas pageant. It was square. She had tried a round one, but the innkeeper kept missing his cue, and with a square moon he could see the corner coming. The dramatic arts occasionally require an adjustment to the heavens.

Across the parish world, Arthur Bell, DDS, retired, was painting a large model tooth for his granddaughter. The Moon and the tooth happened to share a Material. In those days a brush could take this as permission to paint them both.

June circled the Sea of Tranquility. Dr. Bell put a handsome gold filling in it. At the dress rehearsal Bethlehem rose under the most expensive sky in Judea.

Three specimens of the ensuing correspondence survive in June’s exercise book.

| Written on the tooth | Written on the Moon |
| --- | --- |
| DO YOU FLOSS | WE HAVE NO STRING ON THE MOON |
| WHAT DO YOU HAVE | SIX MINUTES BEFORE MUM WANTS THE LAPTOP |
| MY WIFE USED TO DO YOUR PLAY | WHICH PART |

His answer to the third question was a little drawing of a donkey with enormous back legs. Mrs. Bell had worked the hindquarters for twenty-two Christmases. June knew those legs. There was a darn in the left knee where, according to parish tradition, a wise man had put his foot through her.

“Did she ever get to see the play?” June wrote.

“Once a year I told it to her on the walk home,” came the answer. Then, in smaller letters, “I generally improved my part.”

The repair arrived on a Thursday. After it, a dab of paint on the tooth belonged to the tooth. June put a question mark on the Moon. In his flat Dr. Bell drew two enormous donkey ears on a molar and spent an unreasonable amount of time getting the left one to flop. Each sat back to give the other room to answer.

The following Sunday June commandeered the notices at church. Would the dentist who had recently done work on the Moon please see her by the biscuits? Four dentists presented themselves. Three admitted under questioning that they had come for the biscuits.

On Christmas Eve the parish hall put on the play. Dr. Bell got a seat in front. There was the old donkey, a little loose in the seams. There was Joseph anxiously watching for the corner of the Moon. And there on the screen, splendid in gold, was the filling June had painted back from memory.

Afterward she took him home for supper. In the living room she had laid out a square of lining paper and painted the whole lunar surface on it. She handed him a pencil. There was one thing she still wanted for the observatory records.

“Show me where you were standing when you saw it come up.”

He took off his shoes because her mum had just done the carpet. He walked all the way across the Sea of Tranquility in his socks. At the far edge he knelt and drew a small folding chair, and in it a man with a programme folded on his knee, seeing the play for two.
