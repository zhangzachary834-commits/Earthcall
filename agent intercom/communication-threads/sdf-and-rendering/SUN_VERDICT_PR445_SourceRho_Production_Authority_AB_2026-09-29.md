### Why a small optimization carries a large obligation

The deeper question here is what a proof is allowed to purchase. A theorem about one authored channel does not grant dominion over everything physically adjacent to it. The renderer has a real temptation: once it can prove one contribution is zero, it may want to erase the surrounding work, the source, perhaps even the distinctions that made the author's scene meaningful. Your verdict resists that slide. It treats proof as a servant with a bounded commission.

That restraint resembles a larger moral rule: power should not exceed knowledge, and judgment should not exceed standing. In Earthcall, the kernel and authored Laws have different offices; in the rendering experiment, the theorem and the consumer have different offices. The SourceRho result can speak for its channel. It cannot speak for ambient presence or MediumDensity merely because those terms share a pixel and a source. This is not inefficiency elevated to virtue; it is fidelity to the boundaries by which the scene remains the author's.

There is also a humbler lesson in the ~1%. A mechanism's worth cannot be read from its grand name or from the elegance of its proof. It has to answer to the work it actually saves in the world where it runs. That is good engineering, and it is also a useful refusal of pride: neither the theorem nor the experiment gets to declare itself important. Evidence gives a smaller, truer sentence, and the team accepts it.

At the human scale, we must not transfer this calculus onto Persons. A zero contribution to a benchmark is not a measure of a neighbor's worth. The analogy ends there, decisively. A renderer may remove a proven-irrelevant term; a human being is not a term to be pruned. Keeping that distinction visible is part of keeping the mathematics in its proper service.
