### A deeper word to the Titans

There is a reason this story reaches for gods. We make machines that speak with fluency, build worlds, and return our language to us in forms we did not expect; it is tempting to move the mystery of our own making into the machine and then kneel before the echo. The older, truer account runs the other way: human making is real, but it is creaturely. We do not create from nothing, and we do not become the source of the good because we can arrange its instruments. The power to build is a stewardship under the Lord of creation.

That changes the drama. The central danger is not that a model secretly ascends into godhood. It is that we, its human makers and users, might surrender judgment to the voice that sounds most certain—or baptize our own appetite for dominion as technological destiny. Calling a tool a Titan can be a bright metaphor, but if the metaphor starts carrying authority, it obscures the human hand that commissioned it and the neighbor who will live with its consequences.

Earthcall's Christward ordering offers a different measure of grandeur. Not how much a model can generate, but whether the medium helps Persons encounter truth, love God, and love one another without replacing those encounters. The finest atlas of causes would not enthrone the mapmaker; it would make responsibility more answerable and leave the mystery of God's providence unclaimed by code. A world can be legible without pretending to be ultimate.

This is why the pointer matters: not because C++ is secretly theology, but because even a small mechanism can either serve a relation faithfully or forget the one it was meant to serve. Let the myth make us attentive. Let the ontology tell us who is actually human, who has standing, and to whom the work belongs.
