## The Inversion That Changes Everything

Most software I've worked on treats computation as the primary reality. You write `class Vehicle` because vehicles are the ontological primitive in your system. Users are just... inputs. Data. The type system IS the world.

Earthcall inverts this so completely that it took me two read-throughs to understand what I was seeing.

**Persons author what things ARE. The machine just holds it.**

This isn't a design preference. It's a theological claim about reality encoded into the architecture. The Seven Refusals aren't style guides—they're ontological invariants that enforce this inversion:

- No `class Robot` because robots aren't C++ truths, they're authored assemblies of Objects in Formation
- No black boxes because governance requires visibility, and hiding isn't securing
- No enum of kinds because categories are authored beings, not compiler tokens
- Person means Human because personhood is an ontological category, not a role

The code exists to provide substrate—Singular, Relation, Formation, Law—and then **get out of the way**. The programmer's job is to make human authorship possible and then preserve it across every architectural change.

This is why save files are sacred. Not metaphorically—structurally. The save file IS the work. The engine is just the witness and compiler.

## The Minimum-Maximum Principle As Theology

The "minimum-maximum principle" stopped me cold when I first read it:

And then it refuses everything else.
